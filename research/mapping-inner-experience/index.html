Mapping inner experience

Research / Open initiative

An open research initiative from Lujan Creative Agency, exploring art as a way to share what experience feels like from the inside, building on the work of Chilean neuroscientist Francisco J. Varela.

The idea

Science can measure what happens in the brain, but experience only exists from the inside. A scan can show that fear happened. It can’t show what the fear felt like.

From the outside

Brain imaging, heart rate, hormones, behavior. What can be measured.

From the inside

Lived experience: how a feeling builds, where it sits in the body, what it’s like.

In 1996, Varela proposed neurophenomenology, a way of studying the mind that treats both halves as essential and lets each one correct the other. Since then, researchers have trained people to describe their experience carefully and used those descriptions to make sense of brain data.

Most of those methods depend on words. But many people experience more than they can put into words, or communicate in other ways. Art has always been a way to show what language can’t reach. This initiative asks whether art-making can widen who gets to contribute their inner experience to science.

Questions we’re exploring

  • Can drawings, paintings and other artwork work as rigorous first-person reports, alongside or in place of spoken descriptions?
  • How can first-person research methods be made accessible to nonverbal, neurodivergent and disabled participants?
  • What can art therapy practice teach research about describing emotion and bodily experience?
  • How can AI help find patterns across many people’s art and descriptions, while keeping consent, care and credit with the people who made them?

How we work

  • Open access. Everything we learn is published free, here and in an open archive, with no paywalls.
  • Credit everyone. Every contributor is named, unless they choose not to be, and the people whose work came before are cited.
  • Accessible first. Findings are published as accessible web pages.
  • Consent and care. No one’s experience or artwork is used without clear permission, and permission can be withdrawn.
  • Respect the traditions. We draw on phenomenology and contemplative practice with attribution, not extraction.
  • People decide. AI tools can assist, but people make the decisions.

Lineage and credits

This work stands on the shoulders of many people and traditions.

  1. Centuries

    Buddhist contemplative traditions developed attention to one’s own mind as a trainable skill.

  2. 1900s

    Edmund Husserl founded phenomenology, the careful description of experience as it appears.

  3. 1972

    Humberto Maturana and Francisco J. Varela, working in Chile, introduced autopoiesis: living systems continuously create and maintain themselves.

  4. 1987

    Varela, the 14th Dalai Lama and Adam Engle co-founded the Mind & Life Institute, bringing scientists and contemplatives together.

  5. 1991

    Varela, Evan Thompson and Eleanor Rosch published The Embodied Mind, joining cognitive science with lived experience.

  6. 1996

    Varela proposed neurophenomenology as a remedy for the hard problem of consciousness.

  7. 2001

    Varela wrote “Intimate Distances,” a first-person account of living after his liver transplant. He died that year, at 54.

  8. 2002

    Antoine Lutz and colleagues showed that participants’ own descriptions could reveal patterns in brain data that otherwise looked like noise.

  9. 2006

    Claire Petitmengin described the interview method that became micro-phenomenology, helping people put a single moment of experience into words.

  10. 2007

    Evan Thompson carried Varela’s ideas forward in Mind in Life.

  11. 2014

    Lauri Nummenmaa and colleagues asked people to color body outlines where they felt emotions, producing maps that were strikingly similar across Western European and East Asian participants.

  12. 2016

    Girija Kaimal and colleagues studied how art-making affects stress hormones.

  13. 2023

    Susan Magsamen and Ivy Ross surveyed the field of neuroaesthetics in Your Brain on Art.
